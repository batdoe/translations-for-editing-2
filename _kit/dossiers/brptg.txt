DOSSIER — BRAZILIAN PORTUGUESE (Português brasileiro)
Reusable, genre-general dossier for the multi-book kit: cozy mystery, dark mafia romance, sports romance.
Read with TRANSLATOR_CORE.txt — the completeness mandate and "do not improve" rule bind here too.

NARRATIVE TENSE (LOCKED — must be IDENTICAL across every chapter of a book)
- First mirror the SOURCE: if the book narrates in the present, narrate in the present; if in the
  past (the usual case), narrate in the past. Never change a book's basic tense.
- For PAST-TENSE narration, use the tense named below and keep it identical in EVERY chapter.
  Mixing narrative tenses across chapters is a visible continuity error a reader WILL notice.
- PORTUGUÊS (BR): pretérito perfeito simples + imperfeito na narração. Não narrar no pretérito
  perfeito composto ('tenho ido').

REGISTER & VOICE
- Contemporary standard BRAZILIAN Portuguese (português brasileiro padrão culto, but living and natural) —
  literate yet warm, never the stiff peninsular register. This is NOT European Portuguese: vocabulary,
  syntax and pronoun placement all default to BR usage.
- 2nd person: "você" is the default for direct address (not "tu"; never the European "tu" + 2nd-person
  conjugation). "Você" takes 3rd-person verb forms: você vai, você sabe, você quer. Possessive "seu/sua"
  and object "te/lhe" attach to você naturally in BR speech (te amo, vou te ligar, você esqueceu o seu casaco).
  Plural address: "vocês". Formal/deferential: "o senhor / a senhora".
- PRONOUN PLACEMENT (proclisis is the BR default): the clitic goes BEFORE the verb in ordinary speech and
  prose — "te amo" (NOT "amo-te"), "me dá isso" (NOT "dá-me isso"), "se chama" (NOT "chama-se"),
  "me liga depois" (NOT "liga-me depois"). Enclisis/mesoclisis (amá-lo-ia) reads as European or archaic;
  avoid it unless deliberately stiff/old-fashioned. Even sentence-initially, BR prose accepts "Me passa o sal"
  in dialogue. In more formal narration you may use enclisis after a verb that opens a clause, but proclisis
  is the safe, natural BR choice almost everywhere.
- VOCABULARY (use Brazilian words, not European): celular (NOT telemóvel), ônibus (NOT autocarro),
  trem (NOT comboio), geladeira (NOT frigorífico), banheiro (NOT casa de banho), açougue (NOT talho),
  sorvete (NOT gelado), suco (NOT sumo), café da manhã (NOT pequeno-almoço), terno (NOT fato),
  meias (NOT peúgas), grama [the lawn] (NOT relva), aterrissar (NOT aterrar). Filler/flavor: legal/maneiro
  (cool, nice), cara (man/dude), gente (you guys / wow), nossa / nossa senhora (geez), pô / poxa (aw, come on),
  tá / tá bom (okay), valeu (thanks/cheers), beleza (all good), grana (cash), bagunça (mess), saco (a drag;
  "que saco" = what a pain), né (right? / huh).
- GERUND is the BR present-progressive (NOT the European "estar a + infinitive"): estou indo (NOT estou a ir),
  está chovendo (NOT está a chover), ela está dormindo, eles estão chegando. Use the gerund freely; it is one
  of the clearest BR markers.
- Restructure sentences for natural Brazilian rhythm, not word-for-word. BR prose tolerates warm, flowing,
  slightly longer sentences in narration and clipped, contraction-heavy lines in dialogue.

TONE RANGE (flex per book; the per-book note names the tone — match it)
- COZY (mystery / small-town warmth): aconchegante is the keyword — "o aconchego de casa" (warmth of home).
  Diminutives create coziness and affection: casinha, xícara de chá, gatinho, biscoitinho, um cafezinho.
  Wry, gentle humor; the inconvenient is met with "fazer das tripas coração" or a sighing "ai, meu Deus".
  Touchstone: "As aparências enganam." Keep it warm and communal, never bleak.
- DARK / MAFIA (violence + threat + explicit sex): cold, controlled menace and full-blooded profanity.
  BR Portuguese has a rich, unflinching register of threat and obscenity — use it at full strength (see
  PROFANITY ladder). Short, hard sentences for violence; possessive, predatory intimacy. Touchstone for the
  cold threat: "Você não faz ideia de com quem está mexendo." / "Vou acabar com a sua raça."
- SPORTS ROMANCE (banter, locker-room teasing, slow burn): fast, playful, teasing dialogue full of BR
  interjections (cara, mano, relaxa, para com isso, sério?), light profanity for ribbing (caralho de leve,
  "que isso, porra", "vai se foder" said with a grin). Touchstone for the banter beat: "Pode rir, mas eu
  jogo melhor que você." Warm rivalry, never cruel.

DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION (critical — Brazilian prose uses the TRAVESSÃO, follow exactly)
- BR fiction opens each spoken turn with a TRAVESSÃO (em dash —, NOT a hyphen, NOT quotation marks). A space
  follows the dash, then the line.
    EN: She said, "Good morning."                  ->  — Bom dia — disse ela.
    EN: "Where are you going?" she asked.           ->  — Aonde você vai? — perguntou ela.
- MID-LINE attribution (incise): the dash CLOSES before the verb of speaking and a second dash REOPENS the
  speech. Note the BR convention: the attribution verb is lowercase, and if the speech continues, punctuation
  rules resume after the second dash.
    EN: "I don't know," he said. "It's strange."    ->  — Não sei — disse ele. — É estranho.
    EN: "Wait," she said, "I need to think."         ->  — Espera — disse ela —, preciso pensar.
      (the comma after the closing dash carries the pause of the original comma-spliced line)
- INTERRUPTION / cut-off speech: trail the line with a travessão (no space before it).
    EN: "Wait, I need to—" she began.                ->  — Espera, eu preciso— começou ela.
    EN: "But you said—" "I lied."                     ->  — Mas você disse— / — Eu menti.
- REPORTED / quoted speech inside narration (not a dialogue turn): BR uses ASPAS. Default to the curved
  double quotes "…" ; the angular «…» (aspas francesas) are also valid and common in literary BR typesetting —
  pick one system per book and keep it consistent.
    EN: he described it as "completely normal."      ->  ele descreveu como "completamente normal".
    EN: She kept thinking, this is wrong.             ->  Ela ficava pensando: "isso está errado".
- A FIFTH worked example, full mid-line + continuation in the dark register:
    EN: "You think this is over?" he said, stepping closer. "It's just starting."
                                                     ->  — Você acha que acabou? — disse ele, dando um passo
                                                         à frente. — Está só começando.
- Question/exclamation marks sit tight against the last word (Aonde? not Aonde ?). Never begin a
  narration-only paragraph with a dialogue travessão. Keep paragraph breaks 1:1 with the source.

  Body/act vocabulary by heat: beijo / pele / corpo (tender) -> peito, coxa, quadril, gemido, arfar (sensual)
  -> bunda, seios/peitos, boca, língua, chupar, lamber (explicit) -> pau / pica (cock), buceta / xoxota

  "goza pra mim", "mais fundo", "não para". Match the source's exact crudeness — clinical only if the
  source is clinical, filthy if the source is filthy.

GENRE VOCABULARY (English-kept vs. translated, by BR usage)
- CRIME / MAFIA: máfia, chefão / capo (capo kept), o Don / o Chefe, soldado, capanga / segurança (henchman),
  acerto de contas (settling scores), encomenda (a hit/contract), queima de arquivo (cleanup killing),
  lavagem de dinheiro (money laundering), suborno (bribe), propina (kickback/graft), arma / ferro [slang for
  gun], delegado (police chief), DP / delegacia (precinct), perícia (forensics), corpo (the body),
  cena do crime (crime scene), álibi (kept), refém (hostage), ajuste de contas. Keep "FBI", "DNA", "Glock".
- ROMANCE / INTIMACY: paixão, tesão (lust/horniness — strong, very BR), química, vontade, ciúme (jealousy),
  carinho (tenderness), apaixonar-se / se apaixonar, ficar com alguém (to hook up / make out with), pegar
  (to get with someone), transar / fazer amor / foder (sex, by heat), gozar (climax), arrepio (shiver),
  borboletas no estômago. "Crush" is often kept (meu crush) in contemporary BR.
- HOCKEY / SPORTS: BR has little native hockey lexicon, so KEEP most hockey terms in English and let the
  prose gloss them naturally: hóquei (sport name translated/accented), but rink, puck, slapshot, power play,
  hat-trick, goalie, body check, penalty box, faceoff are commonly KEPT in English (italicized or quoted on
  first use, then plain). Translatable / commonly translated: gol (goal/point), técnico (coach), time
  (the team), vestiário (locker room), arquibancada (the stands/bleachers), torcida (the fans/crowd),
  treino (practice), temporada (season), campeonato (championship), goleiro (can stand in for goalie),
  jogador / atleta. General sports slang for banter: "tá voando" (on fire), "pegou pesado" (went hard),
  "relaxa, mano", "joga muito" (plays great). Keep team/league proper names (NHL) in English.

TYPOGRAPHY (Brazilian conventions)
- TRAVESSÃO vs HÍFEN: the travessão (— em dash) opens dialogue and brackets asides — it is NOT the hyphen (-).
  The hyphen joins compounds and clitics (guarda-chuva, beija-flor, vê-lo, café da manhã has no hyphen but
  bem-vindo does). Never use a hyphen to open speech. An en dash is not used in BR fiction; use the travessão.
- ASPAS: double curved quotes "…" are standard for reported speech and quoted material; aspas angulares
  «…» are an accepted literary alternative — choose one and stay consistent. Single quotes '…' only for a
  quote inside a quote.
- REFORMED-ORTHOGRAPHY ACCENTS (Acordo Ortográfico, current): NO trema (so "linguiça", "tranquilo",
  "cinquenta" — never "lingüiça"). Dropped accents on "ideia", "assembleia", "heroico", "voo", "enjoo"
  (no circumflex/accent now). Keep: á â ã à é ê í ó ô õ ú and the ç and til (ã, õ). "Para" the verb form
  no longer takes the differentiating accent (pôr keeps its circumflex; pode/pôde distinction kept).
  Common correct forms: você, é, está, também, então, coração, mãe, irmã, não, então, avô/avó.
- ELLIPSIS: reticências "…" (three dots, ideally the single … glyph), tight to the preceding word
  (Sei lá… not Sei lá ...). Use for trailing-off speech; for a hard cut-off use the travessão instead.
- Numbers/decimals: BR uses comma for decimals and period for thousands (R$ 1.500,00) — but in fiction,
  spell out numbers per house style unless the source uses figures.

CHAPTER WORD
- "Capítulo N" with an Arabic numeral (Capítulo 1, Capítulo 2 …). Do not invent chapter titles where the
  source has none.
