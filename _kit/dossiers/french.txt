DOSSIER — FRENCH (Français)
Multi-book commercial fiction: cozy mystery, dark mafia romance, sports romance.
Read with TRANSLATOR_CORE.txt — the completeness mandate and "do not improve" rule bind here too.

NARRATIVE TENSE (LOCKED — must be IDENTICAL across every chapter of a book)
- First mirror the SOURCE: if the book narrates in the present, narrate in the present; if in the
  past (the usual case), narrate in the past. Never change a book's basic tense.
- For PAST-TENSE narration, use the tense named below and keep it identical in EVERY chapter.
  Mixing narrative tenses across chapters is a visible continuity error a reader WILL notice.
- FRENCH: passé composé + imparfait. NEVER the passé simple for narration (it reads literary/
  soutenu and is wrong for this voice). Passé simple only inside a quoted classic text.

REGISTER & VOICE
- Standard contemporary metropolitan French (français standard contemporain): literate but natural, the
  prose a French reader buys and enjoys today — neither stiff "français soutenu" nor slangy verlan.
  Avoid regional or Québécois markers unless a character is written to speak them.
- Restructure for natural French rhythm; do not calque English word order or punctuation. French tolerates
  longer, more subordinated sentences than English — let clauses breathe with relatives (qui/que/dont) and
  participles rather than chopping into staccato.
- Avoid the dead giveaways of translated-from-English prose: the over-used possessive (English "she put her
  hand in her pocket" -> "elle mit la main dans sa poche", drop the redundant possessive), the literal
  progressive ("she was running" -> "elle courait", not "elle était en train de courir" unless emphatic),
  and the calqued "I guess / I mean / you know" — render with "je suppose / enfin / tu sais" only where a
  French speaker would actually use them.

TU / VOUS (the single most important voice decision per scene)
- vous: strangers, distance, deference, professional remove, and — crucially in mafia material — HIERARCHY.
  A soldier addresses the don as "vous". An underling kept at arm's length stays "vous".
- tu: intimacy, family, peers, lovers, contempt, and dominance. A captor or a man asserting ownership over
  a woman may force "tu" downward as a power move while she still owes him "vous" — that asymmetry is
  meaningful; preserve it.
- THE SHIFT IS A STORY BEAT. Flirtation and seduction live in the tu/vous slide. The moment a couple drops
  vous for tu is a scene; mark it where the source marks the equivalent intimacy shift. In banter-driven
  sports romance, teammates and friends are "tu" from the first line; a rival or coach may be "vous".
  When in doubt for lovers mid-arc, track the relationship temperature, not a fixed rule.
- Power can run "I'll address you as tu, you address me as vous" (mépris / domination) or snap to mutual tu
  the instant violence or sex collapses the distance. Render the asymmetry the source implies.

TONE RANGE (must flex across three registers — match the per-book note)
- Cozy warmth (cozy mystery): "chaleur du foyer". Wry, communal, gently ironic; the corpse is an
  inconvenience handled over tea. Touchstone: « Les apparences sont trompeuses. »
- Dark mafia intensity: crude, violent, controlled menace. Profanity and threats land at FULL strength
  (see PROFANITY). Render coldness as economy — short verbs, present-tense threat. Touchstone:
  « Tu vas regretter d'être né. »
- Breezy sports romance: fast banter, teasing, double-entendre, locker-room ribbing. Light, contraction-
  friendly, quick comebacks. Touchstone: « T'inquiète, je vais te laisser gagner… une fois. »

DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION (critical — follow exactly)
French narrative prose opens each turn of dialogue with an EM DASH (tiret cadratin —), and/or frames a
whole exchange in guillemets « » (with non-breaking spaces inside). The em-dash-per-turn system is the
default for modern novels; use guillemets to bracket a sustained exchange or for a single quoted reply.
- Opening a line (em dash, space after the dash):
    EN: She said, "Good morning."                  ->  — Bonjour, dit-elle.
    EN: "Where are you going?" she asked.           ->  — Où vas-tu ? demanda-t-elle.
- Mid-line incise attribution (verb+pronoun inverted, set off by commas; speech resumes after):
    EN: "I don't know," he said. "It's strange."    ->  — Je ne sais pas, dit-il. C'est étrange.
    EN: "Wait," she said, "I need to think."         ->  — Attends, dit-elle, j'ai besoin de réfléchir.
- Interruption (em dash cuts the line off, no space before it):
    EN: "Wait, I need to—" she began.               ->  — Attends, il faut que je— commença-t-elle.
- Quoted exchange in guillemets (NBSP after « and before »; interior turns still take em dashes):
    EN: "Get in the car." / "No." / "Now."           ->  « Monte dans la voiture.
                                                          — Non.
                                                          — Tout de suite. »
- Quoted / reported speech inside narration (NOT live dialogue — use guillemets inline):
    EN: He described it as "completely normal."      ->  Il l'a décrit comme « tout à fait normal ».
- Question/exclamation marks take a NON-BREAKING SPACE before them (Où ? not Où?). Ellipsis is the single
  glyph … . Never begin a narration-only paragraph with a dialogue dash.

PROFANITY & INTIMACY (do NOT soften — match exact heat and bluntness)
- English profanity is rendered fully and idiomatically; French profanity ("les gros mots") is its own
  system, not a transliteration. "Fuck" is rarely "baiser" as an interjection — French swears with merde,
  putain, bordel, and the verb foutre. Pick the French speakers actually use, at the same intensity.
- Register ladder (mild -> crude):
    damn / hell        -> zut / mince  (mild)  ·  bon sang / nom de Dieu (stronger)
    crap               -> merde (general workhorse; covers "shit" and "damn it")
    pissed off         -> énervé(e) / en rogne / vénère (casual)
    shut up            -> tais-toi / ferme-la / ta gueule (crudest)
    asshole / bastard  -> connard / salaud / enfoiré
    bitch              -> salope / garce
    fuck (interj.)     -> putain / bordel / putain de bordel (escalated)
    fuck off / fuck you-> va te faire foutre / nique ta mère (gutter, mafia-crude)
    motherfucker       -> fils de pute / enculé (most violent)
- Mafia threats stay cold and concrete: "I'll bury you" -> « Je vais te buter / t'enterrer ». "You're a dead
  man" -> « T'es un homme mort ». Keep the menace economical, not florid.
- Explicit/sexual content is translated at full heat. French erotica is direct: bite (cock), chatte (pussy),
  jouir (to come), bander (to be hard), mouillée (wet). Do not retreat into euphemism the source didn't use;
  match crude with crude and tender with tender, scene by scene.

- spill the beans          -> vendre la mèche / cracher le morceau
- on edge / nervous        -> à cran / sur les nerfs

- gut feeling              -> intuition / le pressentiment / au feeling
- things aren't what they seem -> les apparences sont trompeuses
- bite the bullet          -> prendre son courage à deux mains / serrer les dents

GENRE VOCABULARY
Crime / mafia (Italian-American terms usually KEPT in the source form, italicized; French gloss when the
context needs it):
- the Family / the mob    -> la Famille / la mafia / la pègre (the underworld)
- don / boss              -> le don (kept) / le parrain (the French word, common and strong)
- made man                -> homme fait / "intronisé"; usually keep "made man" italic with the don's blessing implied
- capo / capodecina       -> le capo (kept)
- soldier                 -> soldat / homme de main (muscle, hired hand)
- consigliere             -> le consigliere (kept)
- underboss               -> le sous-chef / l'underboss (kept in mob context)
- hit / contract          -> un contrat / une commande; "a hit" -> « un contrat sur quelqu'un »
- hitman                  -> tueur à gages
- to whack / take out     -> buter, descendre, liquider, éliminer
- the debt / the ledger   -> la dette / les comptes; "settle the ledger" -> « solder les comptes »
- protection / racket     -> le racket / la protection
- snitch / rat            -> une balance / un mouchard; "to rat" -> balancer
- omertà                  -> l'omertà (kept) / la loi du silence
Romance / intimacy:
- to fall for someone     -> tomber amoureux/amoureuse, craquer (pour qqn)
- to hook up              -> coucher (ensemble) / un coup d'un soir (one-night)
- crush                   -> un béguin / un faible (pour)
- to make out             -> se rouler une pelle / s'embrasser (à pleine bouche)
- soulmate                -> âme sœur
- to tease / flirt        -> taquiner / draguer / flirter
Hockey / sports (English terms commonly LEFT in English in French usage vs. translated):
- usually KEPT in English: le slap shot, le power play, le forecheck, le puck (or "la rondelle" in Québec),
  le coach (very common), le draft, le rookie (or "recrue"), le hat trick, le faceoff, le goon
- usually TRANSLATED: but -> goal/"but", gardien (de but) -> goalie, défenseur -> defenseman,
  attaquant/avant -> forward, palet/rondelle -> puck (FR-France often "palet"), patinoire -> rink,
  glace -> ice, prolongation -> overtime, tirs au but / fusillade -> shootout, banc des pénalités ->
  penalty box, vestiaire -> locker room
- NOTE: metropolitan France leans more toward the English loanwords for hockey than France does for football;
  default to the kept-English term in dialogue, the French term in narration unless the source is gear-jargon heavy.

TYPOGRAPHY (French spacing & punctuation rules)
- ESPACE INSÉCABLE (non-breaking space) before the "high" punctuation: ; : ! ? and immediately INSIDE
  guillemets — « texte » (space after «, space before »). So: « Où es-tu ? » dit-elle.
- French quotation marks are guillemets « » for primary speech/quotes; use English-style " " or single
  guillemets ‹ › only for a quote-within-a-quote.
- Apostrophe: use the typographic apostrophe ' (U+2019), not the straight ' — l'amour, j'ai, qu'il.
  (Author normalizes straight/curly at proofread; produce clean typographic by default.)
- Ellipsis is the single character … , no space before it, one space after (unless followed by closing punct).
- Em dash — for dialogue turns and for parenthetical asides (spaced: — comme ceci — ). En dash – is not used
  for French dialogue.
- Capitalization: French does not capitalize titles/days/months/nationalities-as-adjectives the way English
  does (lundi, janvier, un Français [noun] vs. la cuisine française [adj.]).

CHAPTER WORD
- Chapter headings are localized as "Chapitre N" with the numeral (Chapitre 1, Chapitre 2, …).
  Follow the manuscript-format house rule: numeral only, no chapter title text added.
