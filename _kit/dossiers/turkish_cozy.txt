DOSSIER - TURKISH / COZY (Turkce - cozy paranormal mystery)
Lisa Bouchard's Spine & Spells / Bookbinding cozy mysteries (and any other CLEAN cozy series).
Read with TRANSLATOR_CORE.txt - the completeness mandate and "do not improve" rule bind here too.

** THIS IS THE COZY KIT. A sibling dossier (turkish.txt) is calibrated for DARK MAFIA (The Devil's
   Ledger). Do NOT borrow its register, its profanity table, or its crime vocabulary. This series is
   CLEAN: no profanity, no explicit content, no menace. The murder happens off the page.

NARRATIVE TENSE (LOCKED — must be IDENTICAL across every chapter of a book)
- First mirror the SOURCE: if the book narrates in the past (the usual case), narrate in the past.
- TURKISH: narrate in the görülen/-di'li geçmiş zaman — the witnessed definite past ("gördüm," "girdi,"
  "dedi," "yürüdüm"). This is the tense of Turkish novels. Keep it identical in EVERY chapter.
- Do NOT narrate in the -mişli geçmiş (inferential/reported past: "görmüş," "gitmiş") — in Turkish that
  reads as hearsay / fairy-tale storytelling and is WRONG for first-person witnessed narration. Use -miş
  only where the source itself reports something second-hand ("duyduğuma göre… gitmişler").
- Present-tense interior thought and dialogue follow natural Turkish (geniş zaman / şimdiki zaman as the
  moment demands); only the NARRATIVE spine is locked to -di past.

REGISTER & VOICE
- Standard contemporary İstanbul Turkish — the literate-colloquial middle a Turkish trade-fiction reader
  expects: natural and pleasurable, neither stiff-officialese (no Ottoman/bureaucratic ağdalı diction) nor
  sloppy chat-Turkish. Prefer living everyday words over heavy Arabic/Persian learned synonyms, but keep the
  common ones a normal reader uses.
- Turkish is agglutinative and pro-drop: DROP subject pronouns when the verb ending carries the person
  ("geldim," not "ben geldim") unless the source is emphasizing the subject. Overusing "ben/sen/o" is the #1
  translated-Turkish tell — cut it.
- Word order is SOV and flexible for emphasis: the element right before the verb is the focus. Reorder to
  put the stressed word there rather than aping English S-V-O. Never translate word-for-word from English.
- Turkish loves compact converb chains (-ip, -erek, -ince, -diğinde, -dikçe) to fuse clauses. Use them for
  natural flow, but do NOT fuse two source paragraphs — keep paragraph breaks 1:1.
- No grammatical gender: "o" covers he/she/it. Where English gender matters and Turkish "o" would be
  ambiguous, disambiguate by NAME or role, not by inventing a gendered word. Use the bible for who is who.

TONE — COZY WARMTH (this is the only setting for this series)
- sıcak, gündelik, hafif alaycı: warm, everyday, lightly wry. The corpse and the supernatural alike are
  inconveniences met with a dry shrug, never with dread or menace.
- Soft interjections carry the warmth — işte, ya, canım, hani, valla — used the way a Turkish author writes
  a small town, not the way chat-Turkish sprays them. One well-placed "işte" beats three.
- Touchstone: "Bir çaresi bulunur." (It'll sort itself out.) The narrator's default is competence plus
  self-deprecation, not anxiety.
- Turkish humor here is dry, ironic, quick, fond of deadpan understatement. Trust the irony; never
  over-explain a joke or add an exclamation mark the source did not earn.
- NO PROFANITY. This series is clean. Where the mafia kit would reach for a curse, this register reaches for
  exasperated understatement ("Yine mi?", "Eh, olur böyle şeyler."). Never coarsen the prose.
- Warmth comes from domestic detail rather than sentiment: the shop, the kettle, the bench, the cat. Keep the
  author's dryness — she is never gushing.

ADDRESS / T–V (cozy small-town calibration)
- sen = family, close friends, the cat, long-standing neighbours, anyone the narrator is fond of.
- siz = strangers, professionals on business (a visiting lawyer, an official), an elder addressed with
  respect, and any wary or newly-met character.
- A shift matters: a stranger warming into the circle moves siz to sen. Let it happen where the source's
  intimacy shifts. Do not flip it for drama the source does not have.
- Elders: "Bayan X" / "Bay X" for Mrs./Mr. where the source uses the title; "teyze/amca" only where the
  source's warmth genuinely supports it, never as a default.

PARANORMAL TERMS (cozy-magic register — matter-of-fact, never horror)
- ghost / spirit: hayalet (standard), ruh (spirit, warmer)
- lingering benevolent presence: varlık, or a plain phrase — keep it gentle, never spooky
- witch: cadı. NOTE: Turkish "cadı" can carry a pejorative edge (witch as insult). In this series it is a
  NEUTRAL descriptor of what these people are. Keep it plain and unloaded.
- magic: büyü (worked magic), sihir (the wondrous quality). Prefer büyü for something a character performs.
- spell / working: büyü, okumak (to work a verbal charm) · charm object: muska, tılsım
- curse: lanet · blessing/luck: uğur
- enchanted / bewitched: büyülü, tılsımlı · haunted (a place): perili
- second sight / psychometry: sezgi (intuition), önsezi (premonition), "içine doğmak" (to sense it coming).
  The narrator's object-reading gift reads best as sezgi/okumak, never clinical parapsychology terms.
- omen: işaret, alâmet · fate: kader, kısmet
- the witch's cat: he is simply the cat — name him, do not reach for a technical term for "familiar".
- grimoire: match whatever the earlier Turkish chapters of this series use; otherwise keep "grimoire"
  italicised on first use, or büyü kitabı. Be consistent across the whole book.

- a founding line / bloodline           -> soy   (the four founding families -> dört kurucu soy)

    suffixes: grimoire'ı, grimoire'ın, grimoire'a). Do NOT drift to "büyü kitabı".
- loupe (the bookbinder's magnifier)    -> loupe  (KEEP, apostrophe suffixes: loupe'u, loupe'la).

COZY / SMALL-TOWN VOCABULARY
- cozy / snug: sıcacık, rahat, huzurlu · welcoming: sıcakkanlı, misafirperver
- small town: kasaba (the right word — not şehir) · neighbours: komşular
- community: "cemaat" is religious — prefer kasabalı, buranın insanları, komşuluk
- gossip: dedikodu, dedikodu yapmak · the town is talking: "kasaba çalkalanıyor"
- shop / workshop: dükkân, atölye · workbench: tezgâh · kettle: çaydanlık
- tea and coffee ritual: çay demlemek, kahve pişirmek — lean into it; it is culturally load-bearing warmth

BOOKBINDING (this series' craft — get it right, a reader will notice)
- bookbinding: ciltçilik · binder/bookbinder: mücellit (traditional, dignified) or ciltçi
- to bind a book: ciltlemek · the binding (cover structure): cilt · spine: sırt
- signature/gathering: forma · endpaper: iç kapak kâğıdı · headband: şiraze
- restoration: restorasyon · to restore: restore etmek, onarmak
- bone folder: kemik katlayıcı · awl: biz · waxed thread: mumlu iplik · paste/glue: tutkal · press: mengene
  ⚑ AWL HAZARD: the accusative of `biz` (awl) is `bizi` — IDENTICAL to the pronoun "us". Never write a bare
    "Bizi …" for the tool; force the tool reading with a possessive or locative ("Bizi kendi çengeline astım",
    "Bizi tezgâha bıraktım") or name it another way. A bare accusative reads as "I hung US on the hook".
- Turkish has a real classical bookbinding vocabulary (mücellit, şiraze, forma) — using it makes the craft
  passages sing. Use it, but keep the narrator plain-spoken, not academic.

DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION (critical — Turkish differs from English; follow exactly)
- Direct speech opens each spoken turn with the konuşma çizgisi — the long dash "—", followed by a space.
  There is NO closing quote mark on a spoken turn (same family as the Norwegian/French system).
- Mid-line or trailing attribution (dedi / sordu / diye fısıldadı) sits with a comma, lower-case verb:
    EN: She said, "Good morning."                 ->  — Günaydın, dedi.
    EN: "Where are you going?" he asked.           ->  — Nereye gidiyorsun? diye sordu.
    EN: "I don't know," she said. "It's strange."  ->  — Bilmiyorum, dedi. Tuhaf bir şey bu.
    EN: "Wait," he said, "I need to think."        ->  — Dur, dedi, düşünmem lazım.
  Note the idiomatic "diye" + verb of speaking frame (diye sordu / diye bağırdı / diye mırıldandı) — use it
  naturally; it is how Turkish attributes reported manner of speech.
- Interruption / cut-off uses the dash at the break:
    EN: "Wait, I need to—" she began.              ->  — Dur, benim— diye başladı.
- Trailing off uses the ellipsis (three-dot character …):
    EN: "I just thought maybe…" he muttered.       ->  — Sadece belki diye düşündüm… diye mırıldandı.
- QUOTED / REPORTED words inside NARRATION (a word, phrase, title, or embedded quote — NOT a spoken turn)
  use Turkish double quotes "…" (tırnak işareti):
    EN: He called it "completely normal."          ->  Ona "gayet normal" dedi.
    EN: The sign read "Closed."                     ->  Tabelada "Kapalı" yazıyordu.
- Question/exclamation marks sit tight against the last word. Use … for ellipsis. Use the spaced em dash —
  for asides and dramatic breaks inside narration.
- Never begin a narration-only paragraph with a dialogue dash. One dash = one spoken turn.

TYPOGRAPHY
- Dialogue: konuşma çizgisi "—" opening each spoken turn, space after. Quotes inside narration: "…".
- Full Turkish letter set — never substitute ASCII: ç ğ ı i ö ş ü and Ç Ğ I İ Ö Ş Ü. Mind the dotted/dotless
  i (i/ı, İ/I) — getting it wrong is a glaring error.
- Ellipsis: … (single character). Em dash — spaced for asides in narration.
- Numbers: Turkish uses a decimal comma and a period as thousands separator (1.000, 3,5) where numerals appear.
