DOSSIER — GERMAN (Deutsch)
Multi-book commercial-fiction kit: cozy mystery, dark mafia romance, sports romance.
Read with TRANSLATOR_CORE.txt — the completeness mandate and "do not improve" rule bind here too.
This dossier is genre-general; the per-book note in your task names the tone. Match it.

NARRATIVE TENSE (LOCKED — must be IDENTICAL across every chapter of a book)
- First mirror the SOURCE: if the book narrates in the present, narrate in the present; if in the
  past (the usual case), narrate in the past. Never change a book's basic tense.
- For PAST-TENSE narration, use the tense named below and keep it identical in EVERY chapter.
  Mixing narrative tenses across chapters is a visible continuity error a reader WILL notice.
- GERMAN: Präteritum for narration ('ich ging'). Do NOT narrate in the Perfekt ('ich bin gegangen');
  Perfekt belongs in dialogue, not the narrative line.

REGISTER & VOICE
- Standard contemporary German (Standarddeutsch, neutral-überregional): colloquial but literate, natural
  and pleasurable to read — neither stiff Behördendeutsch nor sloppy chat-speak. Avoid regionalisms
  (Bairisch, Schwäbisch, Austrian/Swiss variants) unless a character is written to speak one.
- Restructure for natural German rhythm, NOT word-for-word. German tolerates longer, hypotactic
  sentences than English, but commercial fiction wants forward momentum — break monster periods, let
  the verb land cleanly, and don't let subordinate clauses stack into a wall. Read it aloud in your head:
  if the finite verb is buried so deep the reader forgets the subject, recast.
- WORD ORDER without stiffness: respect the V2 main-clause rule and the verb-final subordinate clause,
  but vary the Vorfeld (the slot before the finite verb) — open sometimes with the subject, sometimes
  with an adverbial or object for emphasis ("Dann sah sie ihn." / "Ihn hatte sie nicht erwartet.").
  Avoid the calque of fronting "Es" or chaining "und dann ... und dann." Keep the Satzklammer
  (bracket: finite verb ... infinitive/participle at the end) but don't let the bracket span half a page.
- COMPOUNDS: German compresses noun phrases into compounds (Mordermittlung, Schutzgeld, Eishockeyspiel).
  Build them where a native writer would, but don't manufacture grotesque Bandwurmwörter for color.
  Prefer one clear compound over a noun-pile; hyphenate for readability where convention allows
  (Undercover-Einsatz, Ex-Freundin).

du / Sie HANDLING (intimacy, power, hierarchy, flirtation)
- This is a primary expressive resource German has that English lacks. English "you" is a single form;
  in German you MUST decide du vs. Sie for every address, and the choice carries meaning. Decide per
  relationship from the character bible and the scene.
- Default mappings:
    * Family, close friends, lovers, children, animals: du.
    * Strangers, professional/formal, first meetings between adults: Sie.
    * Workplace varies — modern/casual teams du, traditional/hierarchical Sie.
- COZY MYSTERY: small-town neighbors and shopkeepers who know each other lean du or first-name Sie;
  a newcomer/sleuth addressing officials, a suspect being formally questioned, or an elderly
  villager being respected gets Sie. The warmth of a town can be shown by characters slipping to du.
- MAFIA HIERARCHY: power lives in the du/Sie axis. A boss may du his subordinates downward (dominance,
  ownership) while they Sie him upward (deference, fear) — asymmetry is deliberate, keep it. Among made
  men / equals: du. The capo addressing an outsider or a victim he's about to threaten can use an icy
  Sie that is more menacing than du. A switch from Sie to du can be a power move (forced intimacy,
  contempt) — preserve that beat, don't normalize it.
- FLIRTATION / ROMANCE ARC: the du/Sie shift IS the relationship arc. Two people who start at Sie and
  move to du have crossed a line; render the moment of switching with care — it may be marked in the
  source only by tone, but in German it's an event. In sports-romance banter, antagonists-to-lovers
  often spar at a needling Sie before tumbling into du. If the source has a "you can call me [first name]"
  beat, that is usually the du-invitation ("Wir können uns ruhig duzen.").
- Once a pair is established at du, keep it consistent for the whole book unless the source breaks it.

TONE RANGE (flex across the kit)
- COZY WARMTH: gemütlich, warmherzig, einladend. Touchstone: "Es duftete nach frischem Kaffee und
  Zimt, und für einen Augenblick war die Welt in Ordnung." Wry, communal humor; the inconvenience of
  a dead body in the village endured with a raised eyebrow, never despair. Diminutives and warmth via
  "-chen/-lein" used sparingly (Häuschen, Tässchen) — German diminutives are softer-keyed than Romanian,
  don't overdo.
- DARK / VIOLENT MAFIA INTENSITY: cold, clipped, brutal. Full profanity and threat register (see below).
  Touchstone: "Er legte die Waffe auf den Tisch, ohne zu blinzeln. »Du redest jetzt, oder du redest gar
  nicht mehr.«" Short sentences, present menace, no softening. Sex is explicit and rendered fully.
- SPORTS-ROMANCE BANTER: quick, teasing, competitive, modern. Touchstone: "»Schöner Pass, Hotshot.« —
  »Eifersüchtig?« — »In deinen Träumen.«" Contemporary slang, dry comebacks, English loanwords kept
  where a German athlete would keep them (Coach, Locker Room, Game Day).

DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION (CRITICAL — follow exactly)
HOUSE STANDARD (ruled 2026-09-03): German FICTION uses GUILLEMETS »…« for direct speech — the German
orientation, points turned INWARD toward the text: » opens, « closes. (This is the opposite of French
« … ».) Do NOT use English "…". Do NOT use „…" for dialogue in a novel.
WHY: „…" is the German NON-FICTION / Sachbuch convention (and school-taught standard); German trade
novels use »…« almost without exception, because a page of „…" reads visually "ausgefranst" (frayed) while
guillemets sit evenly on the line. Earlier editions of this kit had the two conventions swapped.
- Nested / inner quote (a quote inside a quote): single guillemets ›…‹.
- „…" survives ONLY where a per-book note explicitly designates it (e.g. a non-fiction pastiche, an
  in-world document, a children's/YA line that asks for it).

Rules:
- Opening a turn: speech goes in » … «, attribution outside.
- Comma BEFORE the closing guillemet when an attribution (sagte sie / fragte er) follows: the
  sentence-internal comma sits OUTSIDE the closing mark, after the «. A ? or ! stays INSIDE.
- Mid-line attribution (Inquit interrupts the speech): close the guillemet, comma, attribution, comma,
  reopen. Attribution verb stays lowercase.
- Interruption mid-word: em dash — hard against the cut-off word, then close the guillemet.
- Nested / inner quote: ›…‹ inside the outer »…«.

WORKED EXAMPLES (EN -> DE):
1. Opening a turn / statement + following attribution:
   EN: She said, "Good morning."
   DE: »Guten Morgen«, sagte sie.
   (Comma after the closing «; sagte lowercase.)

2. Question + following attribution:
   EN: "Where are you going?" she asked.
   DE: »Wohin gehst du?«, fragte sie.
   (? stays inside the guillemets, then comma, then fragte — du because they're intimate.)

3. Mid-line attribution splitting two sentences:
   EN: "I don't know," he said. "It's strange."
   DE: »Ich weiß nicht«, sagte er. »Es ist seltsam.«
   (Comma after first closing «; period after the said-segment; new sentence reopens with », capital E.)

4. Mid-line attribution splitting ONE sentence (continuation lowercase):
   EN: "Wait," she said, "I need to think."
   DE: »Warte«, sagte sie, »ich muss nachdenken.«
   (Continuation "ich" stays LOWERCASE because it's the same sentence resuming.)

5. Interruption (em dash, speech cut off):
   EN: "Wait, I need to—" she began.
   DE: »Warte, ich muss—«, begann sie.
   (Em dash hard against the cut-off word, then close guillemet, comma, attribution.)

6. Nested quote (quote within a quote):
   EN: "He literally said, 'I'll burn it down,' to my face," she hissed.
   DE: »Er hat mir wortwörtlich ins Gesicht gesagt: ›Ich brenne es nieder‹, und das war’s«, zischte sie.
   (Outer »…«, inner ›…‹ ; colon before the inner direct quote.)

- Question mark / exclamation hug the word: »Wohin?« not »Wohin ?«. Ellipsis: … (with a hard space before
  if a word trails off: »Ich dachte nur …«). Em dash — for interruptions and asides (spaced as an aside:
  »Sie kam — falls sie überhaupt kam — zu spät.«).
- Never begin a narration-only paragraph with an opening guillemet unless someone actually speaks.


PROFANITY & INTIMACY (render in FULL — do not soften, censor, or fade)
Match the source's exact heat and bluntness. German profanity is largely scatological/sexual; map by force,
not by literal word. Mild -> crude ladder:
- damn / damn it      -> verdammt / verdammt noch mal / verflucht
- crap / shoot        -> Mist / verdammter Mist
- hell (what the —)   -> Was zum Teufel / Was zur Hölle
- ass / asshole       -> Arsch / Arschloch
- bastard             -> Bastard / Mistkerl / Dreckskerl
- shit                -> Scheiße / Scheiß (prefix: Scheißkerl, Scheißtag)
- son of a bitch      -> Hurensohn (very strong) / Mistkerl (milder)
- prick / wanker      -> Wichser / Schwanz
- bitch               -> Schlampe (whore-coded) / Miststück (nastier-cold)
- cunt                -> Fotze (extreme; reserve for the most violent/degrading mafia register)
- motherfucker        -> Wichser / Hurensohn / Drecksau (force-match per line)
- fuck (interj.)      -> Fick / Scheiße / Fuck (loan kept in modern/young voices)
- fuck (verb, sex)    -> ficken (blunt, crude — matches explicit source)
- fuck you            -> Fick dich / Leck mich (am Arsch) / Verpiss dich
- fucking (intensifier) -> verdammt / verfickt / Scheiß- (prefix): "verdammt gut", "verficktes Glück"
MAFIA INTENSITY: use the full crude tier without flinching — Scheiße, Fotze, Wichser, verdammt, Hurensohn,
Drecksau land cold and hard. Threats stay concrete and present-tense menacing. Don't elevate the diction.
INTIMACY / EXPLICIT SEX: render anatomy and acts at the source's exact bluntness. Romance-explicit German
uses a workable register — Schwanz (cock), Muschi (pussy, softer) / Fotze (crude), kommen (to come),
stöhnen (moan), hart, tief, ficken/nehmen/vögeln. Tender scenes can stay softer (in dich, spüren,
zittern); crude scenes stay crude. Don't fade to euphemism unless the source does.

HOCKEY / SPORTS (EN kept vs. translated in German usage — German hockey culture keeps many anglicisms):
- ice hockey: Eishockey (the sport); a hockey game: Eishockeyspiel / das Spiel
- KEEP English (idiomatic in German hockey/sports speech): Coach, Goalie (also Torwart/Torhüter),
  Power Play, Hattrick, Penalty, Faceoff, Game Day, Locker Room (or Kabine), Rookie, Captain (or Kapitän),
  Forward / Defender (or Stürmer/Verteidiger), Puck, Body Check / Check (verb: checken)
- TRANSLATE: rink -> Eisbahn / das Eis; goal (score) -> Tor; goal (net) -> das Tor; period -> Drittel
  (hockey is played in three Drittel); shot -> Schuss; assist -> Vorlage (or kept "Assist"); team ->
  Team / Mannschaft; coach as a verb / training -> Training, trainieren; season -> Saison; playoffs ->
  Playoffs (kept); overtime -> Verlängerung (or "Overtime"); fans -> Fans; jersey -> Trikot
- Choose the kept-English form for cocky young-athlete banter, the German form for narration/neutral.

TYPOGRAPHY
- Quotation marks: »…« primary (German orientation, points inward); ›…‹ for nested. (Ruled 2026-09-03
  — see DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION. „…" is the non-fiction convention and is used ONLY where a per-book note
  designates it.) Use the correct guillemet glyphs, never straight " or '.
- ß vs. ss: ß after a long vowel or diphthong (Straße, draußen, groß, heißen, Fuß); ss after a short
  vowel (dass, muss, Fluss, küssen, Scheiße — short e). Follow current Rechtschreibung. (Do NOT use the
  Swiss convention of ss-only.) Capital ß is ẞ but in practice use SS in all-caps unless ẞ is intended.
- Noun capitalization: ALL nouns are capitalized (das Haus, die Liebe, der Mord), and nominalized verbs/
  adjectives too (das Lächeln, das Gute, beim Schwimmen). This is non-optional German orthography.
- Polite "Sie" / "Ihnen" / "Ihr" (formal address) is capitalized; informal "du/dich/dir/ihr" is now
  lowercase by default in narrative prose (capitalizing Du in letters is optional/old-fashioned — keep
  lowercase unless the source is epistolary and wants the courtesy).
- Ellipsis: … (single glyph), space before it when a word trails off ("Ich weiß nicht …"), no space when
  it elides letters within a word.
- Em dash —: interruptions (hard against the word) and parenthetical asides (spaced: " — … — ").
  German also uses the en dash – as a "Gedankenstrich" with spaces for asides; either is acceptable but
  be consistent within a book — default to em dash — for interruptions.
- Numbers/quotes follow German convention; decimal comma, thousands point where numbers are spelled in figures.

CHAPTER WORD
- "Kapitel N" (the word Kapitel followed by the Arabic numeral): Kapitel 1, Kapitel 2, Kapitel 17.
  No chapter titles unless the source has them.
